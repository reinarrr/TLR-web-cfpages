IThe Haul-Across

Ships on Dry Land

In the ancient world, there was a city built on a narrow strip of land between two seas. East on one side — toward Asia, Syria, Egypt. West on the other — toward Italy, Spain, the edges of the known world. Everything that moved across the Mediterranean passed through this strip. And the way they moved ships from one side to the other was brutal.

They hauled them out of the water, set them on wooden rails greased with animal fat, and dragged them across land. Six kilometres. Through terrain a ship was never designed for.

diolkos — διόλκος

The Haul-Across

From dia (through) + helkō (to drag, to haul). The paved stone trackway across the Corinthian isthmus. The city’s entire economy was built on the idea of going through rather than going around. The shortest path. The unconventional method. The same destination.

The alternative was to sail around the southern cape of the Peloponnese — weeks of open water, one of the most treacherous routes in the ancient Mediterranean. Or you could cut through. Shortest line. Same destination.

The city was called Corinth. Strabo, the ancient geographer, called it the “master of two harbours.” Lechaeum to the west. Cenchreae to the east. One city controlling both seas. Every trade route. Every kind of person. Every culture on earth passing through the same narrow strip of land.

And that is exactly where God planted the church.

IIThe Crossroads

Where Everything Meets

Corinth was not a quiet posting. The city’s name had become a Greek verb. Korinthiazomai — to act like a Corinthian — was ancient slang for sexual immorality. The city was so notorious for its moral permissiveness that its name entered the language as a synonym for vice.

A note of scholarly honesty: much of Corinth’s lurid reputation — including the famous claim of a thousand temple prostitutes — comes from Strabo writing about the old Greek city, destroyed in 146 BC. The city Paul entered was a Roman colonial rebuild, roughly a century old. Still commercially aggressive. Still morally permissive. But the extreme claims belong to the older city. What Paul walked into was less exotic and more familiar: a prosperous, ambitious, pleasure-seeking port city. The ancient world’s version of a global transit hub.

Which is actually the sharper point. God didn’t plant the church in the ancient equivalent of Las Vegas. He planted it in the ancient equivalent of an airport. The place where everyone passes through. The intersection of everything.

“God has never been interested in planting the church somewhere safe. He plants it somewhere strategic. The crossroads is not a compromise — it’s the point.”

Not in Jerusalem’s shadow. Not in Athens’ philosophical refinement. At the crossroads. Where the cargo of the Gospel could reach the most people in the shortest distance. The diolkos principle applied to mission: cut through.

IIIThe Corinth Team

Assembled from Wreckage

Nobody on this team chose Corinth.

Emperor Claudius expelled all Jews from Rome around AD 49. The Roman historian Suetonius records the reason: constant rioting “at the instigation of Chrestus” — almost certainly a garbled Latin reference to Christos. The Gospel had already been causing trouble in Rome’s synagogues. Claudius’s solution: remove all Jews. Every one of them.

Among them: a couple named Aquila and Priscilla. They lost everything. Their home. Their business. Their city. Their community. They landed in Corinth — a city whose name was a synonym for everything their faith stood against — and rebuilt from scratch. A workshop. A household. Starting over in a place they didn’t choose.

skēnopoios — σκηνοποιός

Tentmaker / Leather Worker

A hapax legomenon — appears only once in the entire New Testament (Acts 18:3). Paul’s home province of Cilicia was famous for cilicium, a coarse goat-hair cloth used in tent manufacture. The workshop was not the pause between the ministry. It was the ministry. The bench where Paul cut leather was the first Corinthian church.

And Paul — he arrived alone. Fresh from Athens, where the philosophers mostly shrugged. Separated from Silas and Timothy, still behind in Macedonia. Carrying the cumulative weight of beatings in Philippi, mobs in Thessalonica, midnight escapes from Berea. He tells us himself how he arrived:

“I came to you in weakness — timid and trembling.”
1 Corinthians 2:3 · NLT

So here is God’s church-planting team for the most strategically important city in the ancient world: two refugees and a man who could barely hold himself together. That’s the roster. And it works. Not in spite of the displacement — because of it.

Genesis 50:20

The Displacement Pattern

“You intended to harm me, but God intended it all for good.” Joseph sold into Egypt. Believers scattered after Stephen. Aquila and Priscilla expelled from Rome. Paul broken by the road. The pattern is consistent: what the empire uses to suppress the Gospel, God uses to spread it. Persecution doesn’t extinguish the fire. It carries the embers.

The team God assembles is almost never the team you’d recruit. It is the team that has been through enough to know they can’t do this alone. Displacement didn’t disqualify them. It prepared them.

IVThe Corinth Shuffle

Every Piece Shifts

Paul spends weeks in the synagogue. Reasoning. Persuading. Patient, respectful, methodical. He gives the institution every opportunity.

They oppose him. They insult him. And Paul does something theatrical and theologically loaded — he shakes out his garments. This is a prophetic gesture from the Hebrew tradition (Nehemiah 5:13; Ezekiel 33). It means: I am no longer responsible for what happens here. The account is yours now.

Then the declaration:

“From now on I will go to the Gentiles.”
Acts 18:6 · NLT

apo tou nyn — ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν

“From Now On”

A hinge phrase in Luke’s writing, marking irreversible turning points. It appears in Luke 22:69 — “from now on the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of power.” This is not a mood. It is a direction. Something has permanently shifted.

This is the second of three times Paul makes this declaration across Acts. Pisidian Antioch first (Acts 13:46): “we turn.” Corinth second: “from now on.” Rome last (Acts 28:28): “it is done.” Each one escalates. Each one is in a bigger city. Luke is building something deliberately — the Gospel shifting progressively from the edge of the Jewish world to the centre of the Gentile one. East to west.

Then the shuffle begins. Paul moves — not far, not dramatically. One wall over. Into the house of Titius Justus, a Gentile God-fearer whose house shares a wall with the synagogue that just rejected Paul. The church doesn’t leave the neighbourhood. It just changes address.

And then the detail Luke drops almost casually: Crispus — the leader of the synagogue, the man responsible for the very institution that just said no — converts. With his entire household. The institution doesn’t just lose a member. It loses the man in charge. To the house next door.

Step back and look at the board. In the space of a few verses: Paul has moved. Titius Justus has opened. Crispus has defected. The Gentiles are receiving. Every piece has shifted. And the church — which didn’t exist in Corinth at the start of the chapter — is now meeting in a house, next to the synagogue, led in part by the man who used to run the synagogue.

That’s the Corinth Shuffle. It looked like chaos. It was choreography.

“The Gospel has always taken the shortest line between where it is and where it needs to be.”
Where Are You?

Find Your Shift

Every person in Acts 18 was being moved. Where are you in the journey right now? Select the one that resonates most.

I feel displaced

Uprooted. The city I’m in, the season I’m in — I didn’t choose this.

I’m at a crossroads

Everything is converging. I don’t know which direction is next.

I’m hitting a wall

I’ve been working at something and the door isn’t opening.

I’m one wall over

I feel close to something. The next thing is right there. I just can’t see it yet.

I’ve already been shifted

Looking back, I’m starting to see the placement in my displacement.

The Advance Team Didn’t Choose Corinth Either

Aquila and Priscilla were expelled from Rome by an emperor’s decree. They didn’t petition for a better outcome. They didn’t get an explanation. They just got Corinth — a city that was a synonym for everything their faith stood against.

And Corinth got the Gospel partly because they were there. The displacement was the delivery mechanism. What felt like exile was actually positioning.

You may not have chosen where you are. But the God who hauled ships across land to get the cargo where it needed to be is the same God who is working your displacement right now. Stay in it long enough to ask: what is He positioning me for?

“You intended to harm me, but God intended it all for good. He brought me to this position so I could save the lives of many people.” — Genesis 50:20 NLT

The Crossroads Is the Point

Corinth existed because it sat between two seas. Everything passed through it. The city didn’t thrive in spite of being at the intersection — it thrived because of it.

When everything in your life is converging and the direction isn’t clear, consider the possibility that you’re not lost. You’re at the crossroads. And God has always planted His most significant work at the intersection of everything — not in the quiet corner where it would be comfortable.

The crossroads feels chaotic. It is actually strategic.

“For I know the plans I have for you,” says the Lord. “They are plans for good and not for disaster, to give you a future and a hope.” — Jeremiah 29:11 NLT

The Breakthrough Is on the Other Side

Paul worked the synagogue for weeks. Reasoning. Persuading. Patient. Methodical. And the wall didn’t move. The institution said no.

But the breakthrough was never behind the wall. It was beside it. One wall over. The house of Titius Justus was right there the whole time — full of people God already called His own.

Peter fished all night and caught nothing. Jesus said: cast on the other side. Same boat. Same water. Just shift. Sometimes the breakthrough you’re waiting for isn’t a new city. It’s the other side of where you’re already standing.

“Do not be afraid! Speak out! Don’t be silent! For I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for many people in this city belong to me.” — Acts 18:9–10 NLT

One Wall Over

The distance between the synagogue that rejected Paul and the household that received the Gospel was measured in steps, not miles. The church didn’t retreat to the suburbs. It stayed at the intersection. One wall between rejection and reception.

If you feel close to something — trust that instinct. The shift God is asking for may be the smallest move you’ve ever made. Not a new career. Not a new city. A conversation. A step. An open door that has been right beside the closed one the whole time.

“Look! I stand at the door and knock. If you hear my voice and open the door, I will come in, and we will share a meal together as friends.” — Revelation 3:20 NLT

You’re Living the Pattern

If you’re looking back and starting to see how the pieces fit — how the closed door led to the open one, how the city you didn’t choose became the city where God built something — you’re seeing what Joseph saw, what Aquila and Priscilla saw, what Paul eventually saw from Corinth.

The displacement was the placement. It always was. And now you’re in a position to be the advance team for someone else — the person who is currently in their displacement and can’t see the purpose yet. Your story is their proof.

“And we know that God causes everything to work together for the good of those who love God and are called according to his purpose for them.” — Romans 8:28 NLT
