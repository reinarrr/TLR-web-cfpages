Acts Journey  ·  Acts 20  ·  Part II

Finish the Race

"The finish line isn't a place you arrive. It's a Person you become like."

Acts 20:22–24 · ESV

22And now, behold, I am going to Jerusalem, constrained by the Spirit, not knowing what will happen to me there, 23except that the Holy Spirit testifies to me in every city that imprisonment and afflictions await me. 24But I do not account my life of any value nor as precious to myself, if only I may finish my course and the ministry that I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of the grace of God.

Watch the message

Beneath the surface — the setting

The twenty miles he walked alone

Watch a quiet, aching detail most readers skim right over. After Troas, the team boards the ship — but Paul doesn't. He sends them ahead and walks the twenty miles to Assos on foot, alone (Acts 20:13). Stott wonders whether this was the real beginning of the long road to Jerusalem: a man who needed to be by himself with God before the hardest goodbye of his life.

Then the decision that tells you everything — he sails past Ephesus (20:16). He bypasses the city where he poured out three years, where his dearest people are, because stopping would cost him the race to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost. He won't even pause for home. That bypass is a face set toward Jerusalem. Instead he calls the elders thirty miles down the road — and what spills out is the only speech in all of Acts that Paul gives to a room of believers. No crowd to win, no court to answer. Just his own people, and his heart laid bare, standing at the midpoint of his race, looking both ways.

Beneath the surface — one whole life

"You yourselves know" — a life with no second face

Look at the three words Paul leans his whole defense on: "You yourselves know." He's calling a roomful of people who lived next to him for three years as eyewitnesses — and you can only do that if the testimony holds from every angle: if the Paul on the synagogue platform was the same as the Paul at their kitchen tables. If the public man and the private man were one man.

That's a word we've nearly lost: integrity — from integer, whole, undivided, one. The opposite is hypocrisy, and the Greek hypokritēs literally means an actor — a man behind a mask, one face for the crowd and another when the lights go down. Paul is the opposite of the actor. There's no mask, because there's no second face.

And that is exactly what Christlikeness looks like before it ever looks dramatic. Jesus was the one perfectly undivided life — the same with the crowd of five thousand and with one woman at a well, the same in the temple courts and in the garden in the dark, the same in his words and in his wounds. He didn't perform the truth; he was it. And you don't build that in the highlight reel. You build it in the Tuesdays nobody records.

The Course

I did not shrink back — a sail furled in

Looking back, Paul names no highlight reel — he served like a bondslave, with humility, with tears, through plots. He "did not shrink" — the word for reefing a sail — he didn't furl a single inch of the truth. Stott calls it his threefold thoroughness: the whole gospel, to the whole city, with his whole strength.

The mirror — faithfulness before it's ever spectacular: the same person watched and unwatched, Sunday and Tuesday. That is Christlikeness starting where no one is filming.
straining forward
Philippians 3:13–14

He turns and the view is hard — the Spirit has told him "in every city" that chains wait. But hear his own letter, written on this very journey: "forgetting what lies behind and straining forward… I press on toward the goal." This isn't only being pushed from behind. He is straining toward something ahead — a runner with his eyes fixed on a line, moving toward the cost, not away from it.

The mirror — "When the days drew near… he set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51). Paul has caught the same expression on his own face — he runs toward the cross because the One he runs toward went there first.
teleiōsō ton dromon
if only I may finish my course

Two words carry the verse. Dromos — the racecourse. And the finishing word teleioō — which is not Paul's word first. On the night before the cross Jesus prayed, "having accomplished (eteleiōsa) the work you gave me" (John 17:4). Paul borrows his Lord's own vocabulary for the end of a faithful life — not "I survived," but I finished what you gave me. And he weighs his life oudenos logou — "of no account." You can't scare a man who's already written his life into the loss column.

The mirror — he says it here at full strength, and again from a death-cell: "I have finished the race (ton dromon teteleka)" (2 Tim 4:7). Same word, once mid-stride, once across the line — and behind both, the cross: "It is finished" — tetelestai (John 19:30).
The line that keeps it true

You run because he already said "finished"

Here's the guardrail that keeps this from becoming a sermon about willpower. Hebrews frames the very same race: "run with endurance the race set before us, looking to Jesus… who for the joy set before him endured the cross" (Heb 12:1–2). You don't run staring at your own feet, white-knuckling your will. You run looking at him — the One who ran first, finished first, and is already standing at the line.

And notice how it ties back to last week: we run because we were first carried. In Part I, grace came down the stairs and carried a dead boy out living; this week that same carried-out life gets up and runs — toward the One who carried it. So Paul isn't straining to achieve a finish; he's running toward one already won. You don't run to earn "finished." You run because he already said it.

The Living Room Circle

Discussion & Reflection

01

The walk to Assos. Paul walked twenty miles alone before the hardest goodbye of his life. Where do you need to be alone with God before a hard thing you're facing?

02

No second face. Paul called the people who lived closest to him as eyewitnesses to his life. Where is the gap between your public and private self — and what would it take to close it?

03

Toward, not away. Paul wasn't only pushed from behind; he was straining toward a Person. What are you actually running toward — a place, a prize, mere survival — or him?

04

Counted as loss. "I count everything as loss… in order that I may gain Christ." What specific thing would it cost you to write into the loss column this week — and what would it free you to run toward?

05

Family prompt: A relay race only works when the baton gets passed cleanly, not clutched. Think of one good habit of faith our family is "running" right now — a prayer, a kindness, a rhythm. What's one small way we could keep running it faithfully this week, not to look impressive, but just to finish the day well and hand it on to each other?

The Send

You don't have to be spectacular. You have to be faithful — in the Tuesdays, all the way to the line. Most of us are running to survive: to get through the week, to come out the other side of this season intact. But Paul wasn't running to survive. He was running to finish — found in Christ, like him — and a person running for that line runs differently. Lighter. Braver. Freer. Because they've already counted everything else as loss, and there's nothing left for the road to take.

Fix your eyes on the One who ran first and is already standing at the line — and run not to survive your life, but to finish it, found in him.
