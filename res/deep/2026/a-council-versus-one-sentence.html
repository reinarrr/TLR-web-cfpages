The Gospel on Trial  ·  Acts 22–26

Exhibit II · Sep 6 · Acts 22:30 – 23:11

A Council Versus
One Sentence

Seventy-one judges walk in united against one prisoner. Ten verses later soldiers drag him out — and nothing changed but one word said out loud.

Acts 23:6–8 · NLT

6Paul realized that some members of the high council were Sadducees and some were Pharisees, so he shouted, "Brothers, I am a Pharisee, as were my ancestors! And I am on trial because my hope is in the resurrection of the dead!" 7This divided the council — the Pharisees against the Sadducees — 8for the Sadducees say there is no resurrection or angels or spirits, but the Pharisees believe in all of these.

Watch the message

Beneath the surface — the wall

What looked solid was only paint

When the high priest orders Paul struck, Paul fires back: "God will slap you, you corrupt hypocrite!" (23:3). The literal Greek is "you whitewashed wall." It sounds like a temper — and it is — but Swindoll traces its root not to the whitewashed tombs of Matthew 23, but to Ezekiel 13, where God condemns prophets who cry "peace" over a flimsy wall and "reinforce it by covering it with whitewash." The charge against a whitewashed wall isn't that it's dirty. It's that it's been painted to look load-bearing — and it will not survive the first storm.

Hold that image, because Paul has just told the council exactly what is about to happen to it. Seven verses later the storm hits — one true sentence — and the wall comes down in a brawl the Romans have to break up. What looked like the united supreme court of Israel was a coalition, held together by a shared enemy and the agreement never to name the one thing its members actually disagreed about. It lasted precisely as long as nobody said that thing out loud.

A fair note — this is Paul, not Jesus

Luke doesn't tidy up the parallel. Jesus was struck at his own hearing too (John 18:22–23) and answered with startling calm; Paul answers hot, then catches himself and quotes the law he's accused of breaking (Exodus 22:28). Hamm's dry verdict: "Paul comes across as a better exponent of the Law than does its official guardian" — but he is plainly a man being made like his Master, not a copy of him. That should encourage you, not trouble you.

Beneath the surface — the fault line

He didn't change parties — he thinks it came true

Notice the tense of Paul's sentence: not "I was a Pharisee" but "I am a Pharisee" — present tense, twenty years after Damascus. This isn't a smoke bomb to save his skin (Stott: there's "no need to attribute to Paul either unworthy motives or an untrue statement"). Bruce puts the mechanics exactly: "A Sadducee could not become a Christian without abandoning a distinctive tenet of his party; a Pharisee could become a Christian and remain a Pharisee." Why? Because the Pharisees already believed God raises the dead. Paul believed the same — he just believed it had started.

And that is the fault line the whole trial runs along. Hamm: the room doesn't divide over whether resurrection is true; it divides over whether it "has already begun concretely in Jesus of Nazareth." Nobody riots over a vague someday — everyone can nod at a general hope that things come right in the end. Christianity is the claim that on a specific morning, outside a specific tomb, it already happened — in a body, with a name. That is the sentence people cannot leave alone. It was prophesied over Jesus when he was eight days old: a sign that would divide, "so that the deepest thoughts of many hearts will be revealed" (Luke 2:34–35). Jesus doesn't manufacture the division. He reveals a fault line that was there all along.

Why this is Exhibit II

The fault line of the whole book

Exhibit I showed a life that couldn't be cross-examined. Exhibit II names the fact that life keeps pointing to — and it turns out to be the exact fact the whole trial cannot settle. The gospel is not "be a better person"; it is a claim about an event. You can hold a general hope politely forever. You cannot stay neutral about a specific grave that a specific man walked out of. The council found that out in ten verses.

And the day ends the way Exhibit I hinted it would: in total failure — no verdict, no allies, the ten thousand believers in that city nowhere to be seen. And that is the night the risen Jesus comes and, in the older wording, "stood by him" (23:11). The man left alone in the rubble of a false peace is the man Christ comes and stands beside — and hands the rest of his assignment: you must testify in Rome as well.

The Living Room Circle

Discussion & Reflection

01

Someday or already. Everyone can accept a vague hope that things end well. Why do you think the specific claim — it already happened, in one body, with a name — is the one people can't leave alone?

02

The whitewash. Ezekiel's wall isn't dirty — it's flimsy and painted to look solid. Where in your life have you been maintaining the paint instead of the wall?

03

Armistice or unity. Is there a relationship or a room that stays peaceful only because a certain subject never comes up? What would it cost to name it — and what might it be worth?

04

Not far from the kingdom. Some Pharisees ended up defending Paul. Who have you written off as "on the other side" who may be closer to Christ than you assume?

05

Stood by. Read Acts 23:11 next to 2 Timothy 4:16–17. When were you last genuinely alone in something — and, looking back, where was Christ standing?

The Send

A whole court came apart over one word, because that word asks for a verdict and not a nod. The gospel will do the same wherever it's actually said. Don't soften it into a someday. Say the specific thing — a real morning, a real tomb, a real name — and let it reveal what a room is made of, yours included.

Nobody riots over a someday. They divide over a grave that a man walked out of.
