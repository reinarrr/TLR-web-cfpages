Paul is in the dock. But it is really the gospel that is on trial.

The hub for the Acts 22–26 series. Six hearings become six exhibits — and under each one, the question the whole case is actually about: is it true?

The Genre Changes

The journey becomes a courtroom

At Acts 22:1 Paul lifts a bleeding hand over a mob and asks for a hearing. The word Luke chooses for what follows is apologia — a legal defense. From that word to the last page, Acts stops being a missionary journey and turns into a trial: a temple crowd, the Sanhedrin, two Roman governors, and a king. Five hearings. Four judges. Two years in custody.

And the charges never land. Not one of those courts can make anything stick. What every hearing keeps circling back to isn't a crime — it's a claim: that a man who was executed is alive. So this series reads each hearing for what's really on the stand underneath it. Six Sundays, six exhibits — and beneath each, the veracity question the whole case turns on. The man in the dock is not the one on trial.

The verdict

Innocent — and still in chains

Every judge lands the same way. Pilate, of Jesus: "I find nothing wrong with this man!" (Luke 23:4). Of Paul — Lysias, Festus, and finally Agrippa: nothing worthy of death. Paul says it himself: "I am not guilty of any crime against the Jewish laws or the Temple or the Roman government" (25:8). Innocent — and he keeps the chains, by his own appeal, because the chains are the road to Rome. Strip away every charge that never stuck, and one question is left that no governor could rule on: whether "a dead man named Jesus" is, as Paul insists, alive (25:19). That is the real docket — and it still is.

The Invitation

For seven chapters the gospel is dragged from court to court and never once convicted. The witnesses can't agree, the governors can't decide, the charges won't hold — and the prisoner keeps turning every dock into a pulpit. Watch the trial run its course this autumn, and notice: the question they can't settle about a certain Jesus is the same one still standing in front of you.

Five hearings, four judges, two years — and the verdict on the gospel is still yours to enter.
