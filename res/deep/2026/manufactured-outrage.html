Manufactured Outrage

Twenty-five thousand people chant one slogan for two hours — and most don’t even know why they’re there. Luke picks one word for the chaos, and it sends us all the way back to Babel.

Watch the message

On Sunday we asked which heart we bring when the gospel comes for our centre. Here we go underneath the riot — to the bank behind the “religious” fury, the single word that ties this mob to the oldest crowd-project in the Bible, and the question the whole scene leaves on us.

One

It was never about religion. It was about the bank.

Listen to what Demetrius leads with. Not the goddess — the bottom line: “you know that our wealth comes from this business” (v. 25). The problem is that Paul’s preaching is hurting sales. Then he reaches for the robes: the temple will lose its prestige, the goddess her majesty (v. 27).

And there was a lot of money to protect. His guild made silver shrines — miniature models of Artemis in her temple, sold as souvenirs and votives to the pilgrims who flooded the city. The Temple of Artemis wasn’t just a wonder of the world; it doubled as one of the ancient world’s great treasuries, where kings and cities banked their wealth. Ephesus even carried the official title neōkoros — “temple-warden.” Religion, civic pride, and the economy were one single thing.

Beneath the surface

“Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” was a real cult cry — and the noble-sounding version of “great is the income from the tourists.” When Paul said handmade gods are no gods, he wasn’t touching belief. He was touching the bank — and a threatened wallet will always dress itself as devotion.

Two

One word ties this mob to Babel

The crowd floods into the great theatre — a stone amphitheatre that seated around twenty-five thousand and still stands in Ephesus today — and roars one line for two hours. Then Luke drops the detail that unlocks the whole scene: “most of them didn’t even know why they were there” (v. 32).

25,000
Seats in the theatre · two hours · one slogan

His word for the chaos is synchysis, “confusion” (v. 29) — and it appears nowhere else in the entire New Testament. Why this rare word? Because of where it comes from.

sýnchysis“confusion”

In the Greek Old Testament, this is the word-group used at Babel — and astonishingly, the Greek translators didn’t spell “Babel,” they translated it: in Genesis 11:9 the city is literally named Sýnchysis, “Confusion.” Luke reaches for the one word that is the name of Babel.

Lay Babel over the theatre and it lines up beat for beat: a self-exalted name they keep shouting; a crowd united against God; the whole thing dissolving into confusion; and at the end — the clerk dismisses them and they scatter, exactly as God scattered Babel. It’s Babel in miniature.

Beneath the surface

And there’s a quiet barb: Luke calls this leaderless mob an ekklēsia (v. 32, 39) — the very word the New Testament uses for the church. A counterfeit assembly that doesn’t even know why it gathered, set against the real one.

Because the same Greek root has an opposite twin. At Pentecost (Acts 2), the Spirit falls and people of every language suddenly understand one another — Babel’s confusion run backwards. Hold the two crowds side by side below.

A fair flag: reading Pentecost as the “reversal of Babel” is widely held (Keener and others), and the contrast is real. The best way to put it: Pentecost doesn’t erase the languages — it redeems Babel’s division, giving unity-in-diversity rather than one forced tongue.

Three

What you’d riot for is what you worship

One calm voice ends it. The town clerk points out the obvious — these men haven’t robbed the temple or blasphemed the goddess; if Demetrius has a case, the courts are open; and Rome will punish a riot with no cause (vv. 35–40). The moment someone asks what it’s actually about, the whole thing deflates and everyone goes home.

Which tells you there was never anything real underneath it — just a threatened interest that didn’t want to be named. The silversmiths didn’t riot for truth. They rioted because the gospel had touched the centre of their lives — their wealth, their god, their identity, all bound together.

Beneath the surface

So their outrage was a diagnosis. It told you exactly what they worshipped. And so does ours: what we’ll defend with disproportionate fury — what we’ll riot for, dressed up as principle — is what we actually worship.

Show me what makes you rage out of all proportion, and I’ll show you your idol. The outrage is the tell.

Demetrius is the original outrage-merchant — manufacture a crisis, wrap your interest in a sacred cause, whip up a crowd that doesn’t even know why it’s furious. Two hours of noise, nothing true, nothing decided, nobody sure of the point. If that sounds like the air we breathe now, that’s because it is. We’ve all spent our two hours in that theatre.

Discussion & Reflection

01

Demetrius wrapped a money problem in religious language — “Great is Artemis!” was really “great is the income.” Where do you catch yourself dressing up a self-interest as a principle — at work, at home, online?

02

The mob chanted for two hours, and most couldn’t have told you why. Where have you been swept into a manufactured outrage — mistaking volume for truth, and agreement for unity — without asking what was really underneath it?

03

Their fury was a diagnosis of what they worshipped. What makes you rage out of all proportion — and what might that be telling you about where your real treasure is?

04

Family prompt: At Babel and in that theatre, a crowd shouted together but understood nothing. At Pentecost, the Spirit made very different people truly understand each other. As a family, where do we settle for just being loud together — and what would it look like to actually understand one another this week?

The Send

The tower always falls into confusion. The slogan always shouts itself hoarse. The made-thing can never carry the weight you keep putting on it. So don’t double down on what was always going to fail you — let it burn, and hand the centre back to the God who was never made.
