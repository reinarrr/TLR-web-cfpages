Watch the message

Beneath the surface — the appeal

Two years on, the ambush is still on the table

A brand-new governor arrives, and within three days the chief priests are asking him to transfer Paul to Jerusalem — "planning an ambush to kill him on the way" (25:3). Two years have passed and the forty men's plot is still live. So Paul plays the one card a Roman citizen holds: "I appeal to Caesar!" (25:11). Read the integrity of it — "If I have done something worthy of death, I don't refuse to die. But if I am innocent, no one has a right to turn me over." He isn't dodging execution. He is refusing to be handed to a lynch mob dressed up as a court.

And notice how the promise gets kept. In Exhibit II the risen Jesus told him, "you must testify in Rome as well" (23:11). Here is how it comes true — not by an angel springing the cell, but by Paul exercising a legal right. The appeal to Caesar is the thing that puts him on the boat to Rome. Providence, again, running through the ordinary machinery: this time, Roman case law.

Beneath the surface — the accidental gospel

The whole case, stated by a man who doesn't believe it

Then comes the gift. Festus, briefing King Agrippa on a case he clearly finds tedious, says the dispute was really about "a dead man named Jesus, who Paul insists is alive" (25:19). A Roman civil servant, bored and baffled, summarizes Christianity better than most sermons. That is the whole thing. That is what the riots and the oath and the two years and the appeal to Caesar are all about — whether a certain Jesus is dead or alive. Everything else in the file is procedure. This is the case.

And here is why it belongs at the center of the whole series: the claim is named accurately by a man who does not believe it and does not care. He has no stake, no faith, no reason to spin it — and he still puts his finger on exactly the thing. The gospel's central claim survives contact with total indifference. It does not need a friendly witness. Festus even admits, sending Paul on to Caesar, that he cannot write down what the man actually did wrong (25:27) — a prisoner shipped to the emperor with no chargeable crime, only a question about whether a grave stayed shut.

Why this is Exhibit V

The whole trial was always about this

Everything the series has traced — the courageous testimony, the split council, the failed plot, the frightened governor — was orbiting one sentence, and here a pagan bureaucrat says it out loud without meaning to. Strip the gospel of everything else, and this is what is left standing: a man who was dead is alive. Not a philosophy, not a moral program, not a private feeling — a claim about an event, in history, with a name attached, that either happened or didn't.

And that is oddly steadying. The case for Christ does not rest on how persuasive his followers are on a given day. It rests on a fact that a hostile Roman could state accurately and still walk away from. Your believing it does not make it true; your ignoring it does not make it false. A certain Jesus was dead. Paul insists he is alive. Everything comes down to that — including you.

The Living Room Circle

Discussion & Reflection

01

The one sentence. If you had to strip your faith down to a single claim that either happened or didn't, could you say it as plainly as Festus did? Try it in one sentence.

02

The hostile witness. Festus had no reason to spin it and still named the real issue. Where have you seen the truth about Jesus land accurately on someone who didn't believe it?

03

Fact, not feeling. "Your believing it doesn't make it true; your ignoring it doesn't make it false." How does treating the resurrection as a question of fact change the way you'd talk about it?

04

Procedure vs. the case. Everyone around Paul kept managing the procedure and missing the point. Where are you busy with the machinery of religion and avoiding the one question underneath it?

05

It comes down to you. The claim survives indifference — but it still asks for a verdict. What is yours, and when did you last actually render it?

The Send

Strip everything away and the gospel is not advice; it's news — a report about a grave that a man walked out of. You don't have to be clever to carry it. You only have to say the plain thing a bored Roman said by accident, and let it ask for the verdict it has been asking for since the first morning: dead, or alive?

A certain Jesus was dead. Paul insists he is alive. Everything comes down to that.
