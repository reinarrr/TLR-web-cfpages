Why This Exists

What Is Kingdom Due Diligence?

In the business world, due diligence is the structured, honest audit a company runs before a major decision — surfacing what comfortable assumptions tend to hide. Many corporations now run a specific version called a Human Rights Due Diligence — an examination of whether their operations and supply chains cause harm to people. The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights describe it as an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time checkbox.

The Kingdom of God has always held a higher standard than the UN. It does not merely ask "are you causing harm?" It asks "are you actively doing justice, loving mercy, and walking humbly?" It asks whether the people in your orbit are being treated as images of God — or as instruments of your agenda.

The masters in Philippi weren't monsters. They were businessmen who never stopped to ask the question. They had been hearing the Gospel on repeat for days — from the mouth of their own asset — and they chose the ledger anyway. This document is an invitation to choose differently before it gets to a courthouse.

This is not a guilt trip. It is a working document — a set of honest questions drawn from Scripture and from the practical reality of how most of us spend our days. It is designed to be run regularly. Not just once.

The Audit

Kingdom
Due Diligence

Six areas. Honest questions. Scripture-anchored standards. Run this on your business, your team, your household, your habits — anywhere you hold influence over others.

Area 01

How You See People

The Imago Dei Standard

Genesis 1:27 establishes the most foundational business principle in existence: every person you will ever hire, manage, serve, or negotiate with carries the image of God. Not as a metaphor. As a reality. The Roman world called a slave a res — a thing. The Kingdom has no things. Only images.

The masters in Philippi saw an asset. Paul saw a person. Same girl. Completely different operating framework. The question this area asks is simple: which framework are you actually running?

When someone is no longer useful to you — their project ends, their season of service is over — do you lose interest in them as a person?

Can you name the personal circumstances of the people who work closest to you — not their job titles, their actual lives?

Is there anyone in your orbit whose dignity you have compromised — through dismissiveness, public criticism, or simply never seeing them at all?

James 3:9–10 (NLT) "Sometimes it praises our Lord and Father, and sometimes it curses those who have been made in the image of God... this is not right."

You cannot honor God and dishonor the people He made. The same leadership style cannot praise God on Sunday and reduce people to functions on Monday. James calls this out with zero softness.

Area 02

How You Pay & Compensate

The Justice Standard

James 5:4 is one of the most economically direct verses in the New Testament. It does not speak in metaphor. It says the wages you withheld have cried out to God — and He heard them. This is not a passage about attitude. It is about the actual paycheck.

In the ancient world, wage theft was a primary mechanism of exploitation — paying less than agreed, delaying payment to maintain dependency, or simply never paying because the worker had no legal recourse. The Bible addressed it repeatedly because it was, and remains, one of the most common ways power is abused in the marketplace. Ministry contexts are not exempt. Especially ministry contexts.

Are the people who generate value in your business or ministry compensated fairly — not just legally, but genuinely proportionally to what they contribute?

Do you use volunteer labour, unpaid internships, or "exposure" as a substitute for fair payment — in contexts where people feel unable to push back?

Is there a gap between what you charge for your services and what you pay the people who make those services possible?

James 5:4 (NLT) "For listen! Hear the cries of the field workers whom you have cheated of their pay. The cries of those who harvest your fields have reached the ears of the Lord of Heaven's Armies."

God sees the paycheck before He hears the praise report. The gap between what you receive and what you distribute to those who make it possible is a theological statement, not just a financial one.

Area 03

How You Use Power

The Servant Leadership Standard

The masters had legal authority over the girl. Roman law backed them completely. But legal authority and Kingdom authority operate on entirely different principles. Jesus redefined leadership at its foundation: the greatest is the servant of all. This is not a motivational slogan. It is an operating system.

Power in the Kingdom is held in trust, never in ownership. The moment a leader's primary concern becomes protecting their own position, they have begun operating in the spirit of the Philippian masters — using the machinery of authority to secure personal advantage. This includes pastors. Especially pastors.

Do the people you lead feel safe enough to disagree with you, raise concerns, or say no — without fear of consequences to their position or their relationship with you?

When a decision benefits you but costs the people around you, what is your default? Do you share the cost or absorb it yourself?

Have you ever used spiritual language — "vision," "calling," "sacrifice" — to make others feel guilty for having reasonable limits?

Mark 10:42–45 (NLT) "You know that the rulers in this world lord it over their people... But among you it will be different. Whoever wants to be a leader among you must be your servant."

The test is not how you feel about your team. It is how your team feels about speaking honestly to you. That single question will tell you more about your leadership than any performance review.

Area 04

What Your Money Funds

The Supply Chain Standard

The masters didn't personally enslave the girl. They purchased access to her gift from within an existing system. That distinction did not make them innocent — it made them participants. Modern supply chains work the same way. The distance between the consumer and the exploited person is greater now, but the participation is no less real.

A Kingdom due diligence asks not just "did I personally harm anyone?" but "does my spending, sourcing, and consumption fund harm to others?" The clothes, the coffee, the technology, the platforms — every purchase is a vote for the system that produced it.

Do you know where the products and services you rely on most come from — and whether the people who made them were treated fairly?

Is there a supplier, platform, or vendor you use primarily because they are cheap — and have you asked why they are cheap?

Where you have a choice between a more ethical option and a cheaper one, what usually wins — and is that a conscious decision or simply a default?

Proverbs 14:31 (NLT) "Those who oppress the poor insult their Maker, but helping the poor honors him."

Oppression does not require intent. It requires participation. The proverb makes no distinction between the person who designed the exploitative system and the person who quietly benefits from it. Both are in the frame.

Area 05

How You Treat Rest

The Sabbath Standard

The slave girl followed Paul day after day. Never off the clock. Her masters exploited her continuously because commodities do not get rest days. Sabbath in the Old Testament was not just a personal spiritual practice — it was an economic justice mechanism. Exodus 20 extended the Sabbath command explicitly to servants and employees. The rest was mandatory. For everyone in your household. Including the people who worked for you.

When God freed Israel from Egypt, the first gift He gave a liberated people was a day of rest. Not a burden — a gift. One day per week to be entirely useless to the economy so they could be entirely present with their Creator. A leader who cannot rest will always — consciously or not — communicate to others that they shouldn't either.

Do the people who work for you — employees, contractors, volunteers — have genuine permission to rest, or does your culture implicitly reward those who are always available?

Do you send messages or assign tasks outside of working hours in ways that make rest practically impossible for others?

Are you taking Sabbath yourself — not just a day off, but a genuine cessation of productivity — or are you modeling the "always-on" culture you may be criticizing?

Exodus 20:10 (NLT) "On that day no one in your household may do any work. This includes you, your sons and daughters, your male and female servants..."

The Sabbath command was designed to protect the powerless from the powerful. Rest was not optional for those who held authority over others. It was a structural command that forced even employers to stop extracting — one day in seven.

Area 06

What You Do When God Disrupts

The Repentance Standard

The masters had an exit ramp. God removed their idol and in doing so cleared the table for a different kind of life. The same hands that grabbed Paul could have reached toward him. The river was minutes away. They chose the courthouse.

Disruption in the Kingdom is almost always an invitation in disguise. Zacchaeus understood this instinctively — he saw the disruption and ran toward it. He didn't wait to be asked to change. He started restructuring his ledger before Jesus finished his sentence. That is what repentance looks like when it is real: it doesn't just change your heart, it reorganizes your balance sheet.

Is there a system, habit, or relationship in your professional life that you know does not align with Kingdom values — but you have maintained because dismantling it feels too costly?

When God has disrupted your income or business in the past, what was your first response — the courthouse or the riverbank?

What would it look like to reorganize your ledger — your time, your money, your influence — the way Zacchaeus did? Not someday. This quarter.

Luke 19:8 (NLT) "I will give half my wealth to the poor, Lord, and if I have cheated people on their taxes, I will give them back four times as much!"

He did not ask what the minimum requirement was. He did not negotiate. He saw the Kingdom economy and immediately restructured. Real repentance does not just change your heart. It reorganizes your balance sheet.

The Alternative

What the Kingdom Business Model Actually Looks Like

The Kingdom is not anti-business. It does not call followers of Jesus to poverty, inefficiency, or naivety about how commerce works. What it offers is a genuinely different operating framework — built on a different definition of value, a different relationship to power, and a different vision of what success is.

Lydia is the model. A successful, independent businesswoman — a dealer in luxury goods, head of a household, operating in a world that gave women like her almost no structural protection. When the Gospel reached her, it did not ask her to abandon her business. It reoriented it. Her home became the first church in Europe. Her resources became the infrastructure of the mission. Her table was the platform.

The World's Model

How much can I extract? People are inputs. Loyalty is leverage. Success accumulates at the top. The bottom line is the final word.

The Kingdom Model

How much can I invest? People are the point. Generosity is strategy. Success is measured by the flourishing of everyone in your orbit. The bottom line serves the mission.

Micah 6:8 (NLT) "No, O people, the Lord has told you what is good, and this is what he requires of you: to do what is right, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God."

This is not a devotional posture. It is a business model. Act justly — in every transaction. Love mercy — in every employment decision. Walk humbly — in every negotiation where you hold the power. Three clauses. A complete operating framework.

A Thought

The masters ran to the courthouse to protect what they were losing. The river was right there. It's still right there. The same God who disrupted their business is standing in yours right now — not to destroy it, but to redeem it.

The question is the same one they faced that afternoon in Philippi: What are you going to do when God interrupts your income?

A Prayer to Take Into Your Week

Lord, search the ledger. Not just the financial one — the one that records how we have treated people, how we have used power, and what we have funded with our choices.

Where we have extracted when we should have invested, forgive us. Where we have dressed greed in the language of responsibility, expose it. Where we have benefited from systems that require someone else to stay small, give us the courage to name it and the grace to change it.

We want to build the way Lydia built — with an open door, an open table, and people valued before the transaction. Reorient our ledgers. Reorganize our priorities. Help us to run toward disruption instead of away from it.

In the name of Jesus, who turned the economy of the Kingdom upside down. Amen.

The courthouse is always hiring.
The river is always open.
