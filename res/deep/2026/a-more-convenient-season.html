The Gospel on Trial  ·  Acts 22–26

Exhibit IV · Sep 20 · Communion · Acts 24

A More
Convenient Season

The most dangerous response to the gospel in the whole book isn't a riot. It's a governor who is genuinely moved, genuinely frightened — and lets two years go by.

Acts 24:24–25 · NLT

24A few days later Felix came back with his wife, Drusilla, who was Jewish. Sending for Paul, they listened as he told them about faith in Christ Jesus. 25As he reasoned with them about righteousness and self-control and the coming day of judgment, Felix became frightened. "Go away for now," he replied. "When it is more convenient, I'll call for you again."

Watch the message

Beneath the surface — the word

The knife is hidden in "convenient"

Felix isn't hostile. He "became frightened." He sends for Paul again and again — the verb in verse 26 is homileō, he conversed with him repeatedly; it's the word behind "homily." Felix probably received more private instruction from Paul than anyone else in the book of Acts. And he never once said yes. Stott's plain verdict: "there is no evidence that Felix ever capitulated to Christ." The lesson has an edge you feel: exposure is not conversion. You can be the best-taught person in the room and still be lost.

The knife is in one word. "When it is more convenient" — the Greek is kairon metalabōn, "having found a kairos." That is the New Testament's word for the appointed, God-given moment — the same word behind "now is the favorable time; now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2). Felix postponed the gospel until he could find a convenient kairos — while the kairos was standing in front of him, in chains. Almost is the most expensive word in this chapter.

Beneath the surface — the mirror

Two governors, two innocent men, one reason

Luke wants you to see Felix against Pilate, and the match is exact. Pilate asked "What is truth?" — and didn't wait for the answer; he "went back outside." Felix hears Paul reason about faith in Christ — and doesn't wait either: "Go away for now." Pilate found no guilt; Felix could find no charge. And the last recorded motive of each is the same: Luke says Felix left Paul in prison because he "wanted to gain favor with the Jewish people" (24:27) — Pilate handed Jesus over because "their voices prevailed." Two governors, two men they knew were innocent, both handed over for the same reason: a crowd had to be kept happy. Neither was hostile. Both were evasive.

Why "self-control" landed so hard — Drusilla

The woman beside Felix was the youngest daughter of Herod Agrippa I — the king of Acts 12 who killed the apostle James. Bruce records that Felix had lured her from her husband; she was sixteen then, and not yet twenty at this hearing. So the three subjects Paul chose — righteousness, self-control, the coming judgment — were surgical. Bruce: "three subjects about which that couple specially needed to be informed. No wonder that Felix trembled." (The couple's son later died in the eruption of Vesuvius — a fact best left sitting quietly in the room, not preached as judgment.)

Why this is Exhibit IV · at the Table

Why God couldn't just forgive

What Felix trembled at was real: righteousness, self-control, and a coming day of judgment. That is the quiet answer to the question people ask about the cross — why couldn't God just wave it away? Because a judge who simply ignores evil is not good; a coming judgment that is real cannot be forgiven by pretending it isn't. Forgiveness that costs nothing would mean the wrong never mattered. So it cost a cross. Paul walked out of Felix's two years alive and bound for Rome; Jesus walked out of Pilate's courtroom to a hill — and that is the only reason there is a coming judgment any of us can survive.

Which is why this exhibit lands at the Communion table. Paul's own defense turned on it: "I always try to maintain a clear conscience before God and all people" (24:16). Before the bread and the cup, Paul tells another church, "examine yourself" (1 Corinthians 11:28). The table asks the exact question Felix dodged — not someday, but now. Don't leave it for a more convenient season. There isn't one.

A companion study

Felix trembled at a coming judgment — and that raises the oldest question about the cross: why couldn't God just forgive? That's exactly where the midweek study, Know What You Believe, goes on the death of Christ — why holiness and love meet at a cross rather than a shrug. The Living Room Circle

Discussion & Reflection

01

The convenient season. Where are you telling God "later"? Name the specific thing you keep intending to deal with "when things settle down."

02

Exposure isn't conversion. Felix heard more of the gospel than almost anyone in Acts and stayed lost. Where might familiarity with the truth be quietly standing in for obedience to it?

03

Not waiting for the answer. Pilate and Felix were both moved and both evasive. When you feel the pull of a hard truth, what's your version of "going back outside"?

04

Why the cross. If God simply forgave without a cross, what would that say about how much the wrong mattered? How does that reframe what the cup on the table means?

05

At the table. "Examine yourself." Before the bread and cup this week, what is the one thing you'd rather leave for a more convenient season — and what would it look like to bring it now?

The Send

The riot never converted anyone, and neither did the delay. Felix is the warning the whole book was building toward: you can be moved, informed, even frightened, and still walk off the page lost — for want of a single "yes" you kept postponing. Don't. The kairos is not coming. It's here, and his name is Jesus.

There is no more convenient season. There is only now — and a cross that made now survivable.
