Why Words Matter

Language Is Never Neutral

The moment you name something, you reveal your relationship to it. A timber company and an environmentalist look at the same forest and use completely different words — and those words tell you everything about what each person believes that forest is for.

Acts 16 is a short passage. But it contains one of the most remarkable vocabulary collisions in the New Testament — a direct confrontation between two completely different lexicons for describing human beings. One is the language of the Roman marketplace. The other is the language of the Kingdom of God. They use different words because they inhabit different worlds.

What follows is a close reading of the key words in this passage — where they come from, what they carried in their original world, and what they still carry in ours. This is not background information. These words are the argument.

"Seven words changed everything: 'I command you in the name of Jesus Christ.' The vocabulary of the Kingdom, spoken into the vocabulary of the market. The collision was economic, spiritual, and personal — all at once."
Latin · Roman Law

res

Latin — "thing" · Legal category for property · The word Roman law used for slaves

Before we get to the Greek, we need to sit with a Latin word that never appears in the text but haunts every line of it. In Roman law, the fundamental distinction that organized all of society was between personae — persons — and res — things. You were one or the other. There was nothing in between.

A slave was legally classified as res. Not a diminished person. Not a person with reduced rights. A thing. Property. An object that could be owned, transferred, hired out, used, and disposed of exactly like any other asset. The law did not recognize the interior life of a res. It did not ask whether a res was suffering. It only asked who owned it and what it was worth.

The slave girl in Acts 16 was a res. That was not the opinion of her masters — it was the legal and cultural consensus of the entire civilization Paul was walking through. When the masters dragged Paul before the authorities, they weren't making an unusual complaint. They were filing a property dispute.

The Modern Equivalent

We retired the word res but kept the logic. Today we use terms like "headcount," "human capital," "talent pipeline," and "human resources." Each phrase categorizes people as inputs to a system rather than ends in themselves. The vocabulary is softer. The underlying framework is the same.

Notice: we don't say "human resource." We say "human resources" — plural, like a commodity to be stockpiled and drawn down as needed.

The Kingdom's Answer to Res The Gospel did not enter the Roman world and argue for better treatment of res. It demolished the category entirely. In Christ, Paul wrote, there is neither slave nor free — not "slaves should be treated more kindly," but the classification itself does not exist here. You cannot have a res in the Kingdom because the Kingdom has no things. Only images.

Hebrew · Genesis 1

tselem

Hebrew — "image / shadow / likeness" · Used in Genesis 1:27 · The word that defines human dignity

Genesis 1:27 says God created humanity b'tselem Elohim — in the image of God. The Hebrew word tselem originally referred to a physical image or statue — specifically the kind of representative image that a king would place in distant territories to assert his presence and authority. When a king couldn't be everywhere, he sent his tselem — a carved likeness that said: the king is here, his authority extends here, this place belongs to him.

When God says humanity is made in His tselem, He is saying: every human being is a royal representative. A living declaration that God is present, that His authority extends here, that this person belongs to Him. Not as property — as a bearer of His likeness.

This means that when you encounter any human being — any — you are encountering the tselem of the living God. Not a symbol of it. Not a reminder of it. The thing itself. A walking, breathing, suffering, hoping image of the Creator of the universe.

The Weight of This

James 3:9 makes the implication explicit: cursing a person is cursing God's image. Not an insult to God's creation — an assault on God's representative. When you belittle, exploit, dismiss, or demean a person, you are not just being unkind. You are defacing the image of the King. That is the theological category James uses. It is a strong word.

The irony James identifies: the same mouth, the same person, can praise God in one sentence and curse His image in the next. He calls this incompatible — not just inconsistent, but structurally impossible in the Kingdom. You cannot honor the King while desecrating His representative.

Genesis 1:27 (NLT) "So God created human beings in his own image. In the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."

Note: male and female. Not male, then female. Not male more than female. The tselem is not gendered or ranked. The wealthy merchant Lydia and the unnamed slave girl carry the same image, the same weight, the same royal representative status. The Empire sorted them into different legal categories. The Kingdom gives them the same word.

Greek · Acts 16:16

Pneuma
Pythōna

Greek — "Python Spirit" · The spirit of divination · Luke's precise term for what possessed the slave girl

Luke is a careful writer. When he says the girl had a Pneuma Pythōna, he is not reaching for a generic word for "evil spirit." He is invoking a specific, culturally loaded reference that every reader in the ancient world would have recognized immediately. This is the Python — the great serpent of Greek mythology that guarded the "navel of the earth" at Delphi.

According to myth, when Apollo killed the Python, its spirit remained — inhabiting the priestesses of Delphi (called Pythiai), speaking through them in riddles and prophecies that kings paid fortunes to hear. The Oracle at Delphi was the most powerful information monopoly in the ancient world. Empires made military decisions based on its pronouncements. Its authority was absolute.

The girl in Philippi was carrying a street-level version of this. Delphi was roughly 500 kilometers away — a week's travel. But the Via Egnatia connected the entire region, and the Python's reputation traveled with the armies. Everyone in that marketplace knew exactly what Pneuma Pythōna meant. This girl wasn't an oddity. She was a product serving an existing market.

Engastrimythos — The Belly-Talker

Ancient writers called these women engastrimythos — literally "belly-speakers," or ventriloquists. It was believed the spirit of the Python lived in their gut and spoke through them. The image is deliberately grotesque: a human being reduced to a hollow vessel. A channel. An instrument with no voice of its own.

She had no voice of her own. Her body was the product. Her masters were the shareholders. Her only function was to keep generating the signal. The spirit used her; the masters monetized the spirit. She was two layers removed from personhood — a res possessed by a Pythōna.

The Modern Equivalent The Python spirit doesn't require mythology to operate. Any system that extracts a person's gift while ignoring their humanity is running the same logic. The enemy doesn't need to destroy your gift — he just needs to monetize it. Keep you producing, performing, generating signal, without ever asking how you are doing on the inside. The Python's business model is still very much in operation. It just runs on different platforms now.

Greek · Acts 16:18

diaponeo

Greek — "to be grieved through and through" · Translated "exasperated" in Acts 16:18 · Paul's internal state before the exorcism

Acts 16:18 says Paul became "exasperated." Most English translations leave it there — which undersells what Luke is actually saying. The Greek word is diaponeo, a compound of dia (through, thoroughly) and ponos (pain, labor, distress). It describes a grief that has worked its way through you completely. Not a flash of anger. A sustained, grinding, bone-deep frustration with something that should not be.

Luke uses this same word in Acts 4:2 to describe the reaction of the religious authorities when Peter healed the lame man. It carries the sense of being troubled to the point where you cannot let it go. It accumulates. It builds. And then it moves.

Paul had been watching this girl for days. Day after day she followed them, shouting. Day after day, the diaponeo built — until it became the catalyst for liberation. God used Paul's annoyance. Which means that the frustrated, grieved, bone-tired response to injustice you feel when you see someone being exploited is not a distraction from the spiritual work. It may be the beginning of it.

The Theology of Holy Frustration

We often treat spiritual work as calm, composed, and serene. Diaponeo tells a different story. Sometimes the most spiritual thing in the room is the person who is no longer able to look away from what everyone else has normalized. The prophets operated here. Jesus wept here. Paul acted here.

The question is not whether you ever feel diaponeo about injustice. The question is whether you let it build long enough to do something about it — or whether you manage it back down to a comfortable level and keep walking.

A Note on Timing Paul waited several days before acting. This is worth noting. Diaponeo is not impulsive. It is not reactive. It accumulates through sustained exposure to something wrong, and it moves when it is ready. The liberation of this girl did not happen because Paul had a spontaneous spiritual moment. It happened because he had been paying attention long enough that he could no longer not act.

Greek · Ephesians 2:10

poiēma

Greek — "that which has been made" · Root of the English word "poem" · Paul's word for what a human being is to God

Ephesians 2:10 says we are God's poiēma. Most translations render this "masterpiece," which is good — but the etymology goes further. Poiēma is the root of our word poem. It refers to something crafted with intention, shaped with care, made to carry meaning that exceeds its raw materials. A poem is not efficient. It is not optimized. It is not evaluated by its word count or its productivity. It is evaluated by what it is — by the meaning compressed into it by the one who made it.

In the ancient world, poiēma was also used for great works of architecture and art — things made to be contemplated, preserved, and protected because of their intrinsic quality. Not used. Not consumed. Kept.

Paul writes this word a decade or so after Philippi. He has seen the slave girl. He has seen Lydia. He has seen what it looks like when the world treats a person as res — a thing — and what it looks like when the Kingdom treats a person as poiēma — a crafted work, irreplaceable, made by God, held by God. The word carries the memory of that collision.

Resource vs. Poem

A resource is something you draw down until it is depleted, then replace. A poem is something you return to — not to extract from it, but because something in it keeps revealing itself the more you pay attention.

The masters in Philippi treated the girl as a resource. Paul treated her as a poem that had been vandalized — not broken beyond recognition, but needing restoration so it could be what it was always meant to be. The difference between those two postures is the difference between the Agora and the Kingdom.

Ephesians 2:10 (NLT) "For we are God's masterpiece. He has created us anew in Christ Jesus, so we can do the good things he planned for us long ago."

Notice: the good things were planned before you were born. Your value is not generated by your performance — it was authored before your performance began. You are not a poiēma because of what you do. You are a poiēma because of who made you. That's the whole point.

Greek · John 8:36

ontōs

Greek — "truly / actually / in reality" · Root of "ontology" · The word Jesus uses for the kind of freedom only He can give

John 8:36: "If the Son sets you free, you are truly free." The word translated "truly" is ontōs — and it is one of the most philosophically loaded words in the New Testament. Ontōs is the adverb form of ontos, the present participle of the Greek verb "to be." It is the root of the English word ontology — the philosophical study of the nature of being itself.

Jesus is not saying you will feel free, or be declared free on paper, or achieve a legal status called freedom. He is saying that when the Son sets you free, your being changes. Your ontological category shifts. You are not a freer version of what you were. You are something new at the level of what you are.

In Philippi, "freedom" was a legal document — a stamp from the Emperor that said you were no longer classified as property. It changed your legal status but nothing deeper. Paul and Silas were in chains in the inner dungeon — legally prisoners, ontologically free. The girl was technically still a slave after her exorcism — legally property, ontologically liberated. The Kingdom's freedom operates at a different layer than the Empire's.

The Question Ontōs Asks

Are you free? Not legally. Not theoretically. Not "on paper." Ontōs — in the actual texture of your being, in the way you move through the world, in what you believe about your own worth when no one is watching and nothing is performing.

If your worth is tied to your utility — your output, your productivity, your usefulness to others — then you are not ontōs free. You are a res who has been told to feel good about being one. The Son's freedom goes deeper than that. It changes what you believe you are — not just what you are allowed to do.

John 8:36 (NLT) "So if the Son sets you free, you are truly free."

Jesus spoke this to people who insisted they had never been enslaved — because of their lineage, their identity, their history. Jesus didn't argue with their legal status. He diagnosed their actual condition: anyone who sins is a slave to sin, regardless of what the documentation says. And then He offered the only freedom that goes deep enough to matter. Not a new legal category. A new being.

Putting It Together

Two Vocabularies. One Collision.

The story of Acts 16:16–24 is a collision between two complete systems for describing human beings. On one side: res, Pneuma Pythōna, asset, signal, revenue stream. On the other: tselem, poiēma, image, poem, ontōs free.

Paul walked into the marketplace carrying the second vocabulary into the territory of the first — and the first vocabulary fought back with everything it had. A mob. A beating. An inner dungeon. The market does not give up its language quietly.

But here is what the passage records: by the end of it, a girl who had been a res was free. And Paul and Silas, beaten and chained in the dark, were singing. The vocabulary of the Kingdom had done what it always does — it told the truth about what people actually are, and that truth turned out to be stronger than the cell.

The Market's Vocabulary

Res — thing, property, asset

Pneuma Pythōna — a spirit to be monetized

Signal — the extractable gift

Revenue stream — the person reduced to their output

Useless — what she became when the signal stopped

The Kingdom's Vocabulary

Tselem — image, royal representative, bearer of God's likeness

Diaponeo — grief that moves toward justice

Poiēma — poem, masterpiece, crafted with intention

Restoration — what Paul performed, not just exorcism

Ontōs free — free at the level of being, not just documentation

A Thought

You don't know her name. Luke doesn't give it to us. She shouted Paul's name for days, and history never returned the favor.

But here's what we do know: somewhere in Philippi, on an ordinary afternoon, a girl heard silence for the first time. No voice in her gut. No masters waiting. No signal to run. Just quiet. Just her. Just free."

"God knows her name. That was always enough.

A Prayer to Take Home

Heavenly Father, we confess: there are times we have looked at our neighbors, our coworkers, and even our families through the lens of utility. We have seen revenue streams where we should have seen souls.

Right now, we ask for that dianoigō moment — the divine unbolting. For those of us feeling the weight of production pressure, remind us that we are Your poiēma — Your poem — not Your human resource.

Teach us the Kingdom's vocabulary. Let us see tselem where the market sees res. Let us feel diaponeo where the world feels nothing. Let us call people poiēma when the system calls them assets.

And let us live ontōs free — truly, actually, in reality free — because the Son has set us free and that changes everything.

In the name of Jesus, the Chain-Breaker. Amen.
